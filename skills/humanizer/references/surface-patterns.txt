SURFACE PATTERNS: AI-TYPICAL WRITING AT THE SENTENCE AND FORMATTING LEVEL
=========================================================================

Source: the Wikipedia editors' guide "Signs of AI writing"
(en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), paraphrased.
That page is a community guide to spotting LLM text in encyclopedia articles.
It is the best-maintained catalog of surface tells that exists, but it was
written for one genre. Translate before applying: a travel blog is allowed to
sound like a travel blog, and a grant proposal is allowed to argue for
significance. The tells below are about formula and vagueness, not about
register.

Why these patterns exist. An LLM predicts the most likely continuation, so it
drifts toward what applies to the widest range of cases. Specific, rare,
checkable facts get replaced by generic, positive, unfalsifiable ones. The
Wikipedia guide's example: "inventor of the first train-coupling device" turns
into "a revolutionary titan of industry". The subject becomes simultaneously
less specific and more exaggerated. Most fixes below are the same move in
reverse: put the specific thing back.

Density is what matters. One "crucial" proves nothing. A paragraph carrying a
puffed-up significance claim, a weasel attribution, a participle tail and a
triplet is a different matter. These words co-occur: where there is one, look
for others.


A. SIGNIFICANCE INFLATION AND PUFFERY
=====================================

A1. Legacy and broader trends
-----------------------------
The most reliable expository tell. The text asserts that some arbitrary
detail represents, contributes to, or reflects something larger, with no
source saying so.

Watch: stands as, serves as, is a testament to, is a reminder, a crucial /
pivotal / vital / significant / key role, a crucial / pivotal / key moment,
underscores its importance, highlights its significance, reflects broader,
symbolizing its ongoing / enduring / lasting, contributing to the broader,
setting the stage for, marking a pivotal moment, represents a shift, marks a
shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark,
deeply rooted, enduring legacy, transformative power, cemented its place,
solidified its role, part of a broader movement, cornerstone of.

Also watch the debate variant, where a subject is placed inside a discourse
that no source shows exists: generated debate, sparked discussion, prompted
broader reflection, raising philosophical questions, shaped emerging policy
discussions, has participated in public discussions.

Two sub-habits worth knowing:
  - Hedge-then-inflate. "Though it saw only limited application, it
    contributes to the broader history of early aviation engineering." The
    hedge does not rescue the claim.
  - Mundane subjects get it too. Etymology sections and population figures
    end up "highlighting the enduring legacy" of something.
  - Species and place articles over-connect the subject to its ecosystem and
    belabor conservation status and preservation efforts even when the status
    is unknown and no efforts exist.

Fix: delete the claim, or replace it with the specific fact that would
justify it and a source. If no source supports any significance claim, the
sentence ends earlier than you expected. That is usually an improvement. When
significance genuinely belongs (a proposal, a review, an argument), state who
holds the view and on what evidence, and mark the gap as
[ADD: source for this claim?] rather than inventing one.

A2. Canned notability and media-coverage padding
------------------------------------------------
The text argues that the subject matters by describing the sources that
mention it, and classifying those sources. More common in output from tools
released in 2025 and later. The distinguishing feature is that attention sits
on the characteristics of the sources themselves: their independence, their
tier in the media landscape, whether they can be found by searching.

Watch: independent coverage, local / regional / national media outlets,
music / business / tech outlets, trade publications, cited in, featured in,
profiled in, written by a leading expert, active social media presence,
maintains a strong digital presence, was identified by, significant and
sustained coverage, substantial secondary coverage, not trivial mentions,
widely read outlets, syndicated through, regional press coverage, documented
in archived programs, peer-reviewed sources.

Note: press releases have cited news clippings for decades, so this is not
AI-specific by itself. The AI version uses the phrasing above and often
echoes the wording of notability rules, as though the case for including the
text had leaked into the text.

Fix: cut the meta-argument. Report what the source says, and cite it. "Wired
profiled her work on AI governance" carries information; "her insights have
been featured in Wired and other prominent media outlets" carries almost
none. Remove "active social media presence" outright unless the account
itself is the subject.

A3. Superficial participle analysis
-----------------------------------
A present participle clause bolted to the end of a sentence, editorializing
about what the preceding fact shows. This is the single highest-value edit in
most expository drafts: the clauses are easy to spot, carry no information,
and are usually unsourced interpretation.

Watch (sentence-final, after a comma): highlighting ..., underscoring ...,
emphasizing ..., reflecting ..., symbolizing ..., showcasing ...,
demonstrating ..., illustrating ..., cementing ..., ensuring ...,
contributing to ..., cultivating ..., fostering ..., encompassing ...,
enhancing ..., reinforcing ..., positioning ... as, making it ...
Also: valuable insights, align with, resonate with.

Retrieval-capable models make this worse by attaching the invented
interpretation to a real name: "Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting
influence", whether or not Ebert said anything close.

Fix: delete the clause. Check whether the sentence lost anything; usually it
did not. If the interpretation matters, promote it to its own sentence with
an attributed source, or mark [ADD: who says this?]. If a named source is
attached, verify the source actually says it before keeping it.

A4. Promotional tone
--------------------
Output drifts into advertisement or travel-guide register even when asked for
neutral prose, and even when the writer has no promotional intent. This also
happens during rewrites: an edit claiming to remove promotional tone can
introduce it.

Watch: boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies,
commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking,
renowned, featuring, diverse array, breathtaking, stunning, picturesque,
charming, bustling, thriving, world-class, state-of-the-art, premier,
unparalleled, must-visit, gateway to, glimpse into, hidden gem, worth
visiting.

Note: older models (GPT-4 era) were blatantly positive. Newer ones are
subtly positive and avoid obvious superlatives like "the best", so absence of
"the best" is not reassurance. Not all promotional writing is AI; the tell is
that the same small set of promotional phrases appears regardless of topic.

Fix: replace evaluation with description. "Nestled in the heart of the
region, the town boasts a rich cultural heritage" becomes "The town is 40 km
south of X. Its 12th-century church and annual textile fair draw ..." plus
whatever is actually true and sourced. Keep the concrete nouns, drop the
adjectives that rate rather than describe.


B. VAGUE ATTRIBUTION
====================

B1. Weasel authorities
----------------------
Opinions get attributed to an unnamed collective, and the number of holders
is inflated. One source becomes "scholars"; two become "widely regarded".

Watch: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics
argue, several sources, several publications (when few are cited), many
scholars, described in scholarship, modern researchers treat, according to
sources, is widely regarded, it is generally accepted, commentators have,
analysts say, reviewers praised, publications such as, outlets such as,
is often cited as, such as (placed before a list that is in fact exhaustive).

Fix: name the holder, or cut the claim. If one paper says it, say which paper.
If the list is exhaustive, drop "such as". If you cannot identify who holds
the view, mark [ADD: which experts? cite one] rather than keeping the
collective.

B2. Vague connection and association
------------------------------------
Instead of stating a relationship, the text gestures at one. This one is
worth hunting because it often hides the fact that the relationship was never
established.

Watch: in connection with, connected with, connected to, in association with,
associated with, particularly associated, widely associated, was linked to,
has ties to, sources identified X as being associated with.

Fix: state the relationship with a role and a date, or remove it. "In 2017,
sources identified John Doe as being associated with leadership of
ExampleCorp" becomes "Doe was ExampleCorp's CEO from 2017 to 2020" if that is
true and sourced, and becomes nothing if it is not. Indirection alone is not
proof of AI, but it is nearly always worse writing.

B3. Knowledge-gap speculation
-----------------------------
The model reports the limits of its own retrieval as though that were a fact
about the world, then speculates into the gap.

Watch: up to my last training update, as of my last knowledge update, while
specific details are limited, while specific details are scarce, not widely
available, not widely documented, not publicly disclosed, in the provided
sources, in the available search results, based on available information.
Also the biography variants: maintains a low profile, keeps personal details
private, prefers to keep their private life out of the public eye.

The claim that something is undocumented is itself speculation, and the
"likely" material that follows is invention.

Fix: delete the whole construction, including the speculation it introduces.
If the absence genuinely matters, say what you looked in and found nothing:
"No birth date appears in [source]." Otherwise say nothing. Never keep a
"likely" sentence.


C. FORMULAIC STRUCTURE
======================

C1. Challenges and future prospects
-----------------------------------
A closing section that opens by conceding difficulty and closes on vague
optimism. The tell is the rigid formula, not the mention of challenges.

Watch: Despite its [positive adjective], X faces several challenges, Despite
these challenges, Challenges and Legacy, Challenges and Opportunities,
Future Outlook, Future Prospects, Future Directions, looking ahead, as the
landscape continues to evolve, positions them as critical components,
continues to thrive.

Fix: delete the section, or replace it with named specifics: which constraint,
measured how, according to whom, and what follows. If the draft needs a
forward-looking section, it needs dated commitments and named actors, not
"ongoing initiatives".

C2. Restating summaries
-----------------------
Paragraphs and sections that end by restating what they just said, and
"Conclusion" sections in documents that do not need one. Chiefly an older-model
habit, still common in longer generated output.

Watch: In summary, In conclusion, Overall, To summarize, Taken together,
As discussed above, This section has shown.

Fix: cut the restatement. If a summary earns its place (a long report, an
abstract), make it carry the findings and numbers rather than the shape of the
argument.

C3. Heading problems
--------------------
  - Title case in headings: "Impact of Technology and Digitalization".
    Most house styles use sentence case.
  - A top-level heading repeating the document's own title, because the model
    does not assume a title already exists.
  - Headings that contain only other headings, with no text between them.
  - Skipped heading levels, for instance jumping straight to level 3.
  - Overuse of level 1 headings, often from Markdown converted badly.
  - Thematic breaks (a horizontal rule) between every section.
  - "X and Y" headings generally, and "Awards and recognition" or
    "Recognition" in particular, which is close to ubiquitous in generated
    biographies.

Fix: sentence case; drop the duplicated title; give every heading its own
prose or merge it upward; make levels consecutive; delete decorative rules;
rename "Awards and recognition" to whatever the section actually contains, or
fold two thin sections into one.

C4. Inline bold-header lists
----------------------------
A list where each item starts with a bolded label, a colon, then a clause.
Inherited from readmes, slide decks and listicles.

Watch: "- **Scope:** ...", "- **Method**: ...", "1. **Background:** ...".
Also non-standard bullet characters (a bullet glyph, hyphen, en dash, hash or
emoji used as a list marker) and explicit "1." numbering where the format
provides numbering already.

Fix: if the items are parallel data, use a table or a real list without the
bold. If they are argument, write prose. Keep the bold label pattern only
where it is a genuine convention of the format, such as a changelog or API
reference.

C5. Unusual tables
------------------
Small, minimally formatted tables holding two or three facts that belong in a
sentence or an infobox.

Fix: inline anything under about four rows unless the reader will scan or sort
it. Keep tables that carry parallel data across several dimensions.


D. AI VOCABULARY
================

Studies consistently find LLMs overusing a specific, small vocabulary. These
words began appearing far more often in text produced after late 2022, and
they cluster: an edit introducing many of them many times is among the
strongest available tells.

Read this section literally. A word being overused by AI does not make its
synonyms suspect. Keep context: "underscore" can mean a literal underline or
incidental music, and "landscape" can be land.

Core list: additionally (especially sentence-initial), align with, boasts
(meaning has), bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring,
enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate,
intricacies, key (as an adjective), landscape (as an abstract noun),
meticulous, meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry (as an abstract
noun), testament, underscore (as a verb), valuable, vibrant.

By era. Not hard cutoffs, but a guide to how earlier and later output reads:
  - 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4 era): additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial,
    delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate, intricacies, interplay,
    key, landscape, meticulous, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry,
    testament, valuable, vibrant.
  - Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o era): align with, bolstered, crucial,
    emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal,
    showcasing, underscore, vibrant.
  - Mid-2025 onward (GPT-5 era): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting,
    showcasing, plus the notability and media-coverage vocabulary in A2.
    The list narrows over time, so recent output leans on fewer words and
    more on structure. Do not treat a clean vocabulary scan as a clean bill.

Grok: distinctively overuses superficially scientific vocabulary such as
causal, empirical and correlate, and still overuses underscore as of 2026.

Fix: substitute the plain word that fits the sentence, one at a time, choosing
by meaning rather than by thesaurus. Do not swap a whole column of them for a
single alternative; that produces a new tell (see E4). Often the right move is
deletion: "crucial" and "key" usually modify nothing, and the sentence reads
better without a rating.


E. GRAMMAR AND SYNTAX
=====================

E1. Copula avoidance
--------------------
LLM text avoids plain "is" and "are", replacing them with agentive or
marketing verbs. One study measured a drop of more than 10 percent in the use
of "is" and "are" in academic writing in 2023, with no comparable movement
before that. The same decline shows up on Wikipedia, and is especially visible
in AI copyedits, which "improve" plain sentences in this direction.

Watch: serves as, stands as, marks, functions as, operates as, acts as,
represents a, boasts, features, maintains, offers, refers to, holds the
distinction of being, is characterized by. Also the elaborated forms common in
recent output: "ventured into politics as a candidate" for "was a candidate",
"began his career as" for "was".

Do not confuse this with "has" in a perfect construction, as in "has been
featured".

Fix: restore is, are, was, has. "Gallery 825 serves as the association's
exhibition space" becomes "Gallery 825 is the association's exhibition space".
"The gallery features four separate spaces" becomes "The gallery has four
spaces". In a lead sentence, "X refers to" usually wants to be "X is".

E2. Negative parallelism
------------------------
Constructions that set up a wrong or incomplete reading and then correct it,
as though the reader had to be talked out of something. Common in human
writing too, but stereotypical of AI when frequent. Three forms:

  - Not just X, but also Y. "Not only ... but ...", "It is not just ..., it's
    ...", "should be encouraged, not belittled".
  - Not X, but Y. "It's not ..., it's ...", "no ..., no ..., just ...".
  - Y rather than X. The reversed form, particularly common in Grok output:
    "prioritizing empirical consolidation of power rather than ideological
    purity".

Fix: state the claim once, positively. "The programme is not just a training
scheme but a pipeline into the trades" becomes "The programme places
graduates directly into apprenticeships". If the contrast is real and
load-bearing, keep one instance and make the rejected reading one somebody
actually holds.

E3. Rule of three
-----------------
Three-item lists everywhere: three adjectives, three short phrases, three
clauses. Often used to make a superficial analysis look comprehensive. The
tell is strongest where nobody would bother with a flourish, such as a commit
message or a status note.

Fix: keep the items that carry distinct information and drop the rest. Two is
usually the honest number, and one is often enough. Vary list length across a
document. Watch for triplets in which the third item is a category ("and
other factors", "and more"), which signals the list was padded to three.

E4. Elegant variation
---------------------
Cycling synonyms for a repeated referent, so one thing is "non-conformist
artists", then "like-minded artists", then "these artists", then "Russian
avant-garde artists". Older models had repetition penalties that encouraged
it. Now mostly a historical tell, and one you can easily reintroduce by
editing.

Caveat: writers taught in some school systems, including Italian, are trained
to avoid repetition. This is not a reliable tell about a person.

Fix: pick one name for a thing and reuse it. Repetition of a noun is normal
English and reads as confident.

E5. What human syntax looks like
--------------------------------
Over 25 years of Wikipedia text, these are more common in human writing than
in AI output. Restoring them is the most effective phrasing-level move
available, because it works with the grain of ordinary English rather than
adding new ornament.

  - Simple is and has phrases: there is a, it has a.
  - Plain words where a stiff synonym exists: wrote over authored, moved over
    relocated, used over utilized, tried over attempted, died over passed
    away, bought over acquired, sent over transmitted.
  - Superlative and definitive statements, where true and sourced: one of the
    best, is the only, was the first. AI hedges away from commitment; humans
    commit.
  - Hedging qualifiers and intensifiers: very, perhaps, tends to, roughly,
    about, mostly. A real hedge in the right place reads human; uniform
    confidence does not.
  - Ordinary wordy constructions, in isolation: as a result of, in order to,
    all of the, a part of, the fact that. Do not scrub these on principle.

Fix: read the draft looking for places where the plain construction was
avoided, and put it back. This is where most of the "reads like a person"
effect comes from.


F. FORMATTING AND TYPOGRAPHY
============================

F1. Em dashes
-------------
LLM output uses em dashes more often than non-professional human writing in
the same genre, and in slots where a comma, colon, parenthesis or full stop
would be ordinary. The AI habit is formulaic: dashes used to punch up a
clause or stage a parallelism. AI em dashes are usually surrounded by spaces,
which most human em dash users do not do.

Two facts that matter for 2026 editing:
  - A July 2026 study found that of contemporary models, only Claude used em
    dashes more than professional writers; ChatGPT used them less. So heavy
    em dash use points at Claude specifically, and light use is not evidence
    of human authorship.
  - OpenAI suppressed the habit in GPT-5.1, and the Wikipedia guide now notes
    that this sign may be heading for its historical-indicators list, since it
    is getting rarer in current output.

This tell is weak alone, stronger in combination, and much more common in
discussion-style text than in formal article prose.

Fix: replace most of them. A comma for an aside, a colon before a list or an
explanation, parentheses for a true aside, a full stop where the clause is
really a sentence. Keep a small number where the interruption is genuinely
abrupt. If you keep any, close them up against the words unless your house
style spaces them.

F2. Boldface
------------
Mechanical emphasis: every instance of a chosen term bolded, or a "key
takeaways" scatter across a paragraph. Inherited from readmes, fan wikis,
how-tos, sales pitches, slide decks and listicles. Some newer models are
instructed to hold back, so absence proves nothing.

Fix: bold almost nothing in prose. Keep it for a defined term at first use,
or for labels in a reference format that expects them. If several phrases in
one paragraph are bold, the emphasis has cancelled out.

F3. Emoji as formatting
-----------------------
Emoji decorating headings or bullet markers. Mostly seen in chat-style text
and summaries, rarer in current output.

Fix: delete them from headings and list markers. Keep them only where the
channel makes them normal and the writer would use them anyway.

F4. Curly quotation marks and apostrophes
-----------------------------------------
ChatGPT and DeepSeek typically produce curly quotes and curly apostrophes.
Gemini and Claude typically do not. The more telling pattern is
inconsistency: curly and straight marks mixed inside one passage.

Curly quotes prove nothing on their own. Chicago style uses them, Word's
smart quotes produce them, macOS and iOS do it by default, grammar tools do
it, professionally typeset publications use them, and citation tools copy
them out of page titles.

Fix: make quote style consistent, following the house style if there is one.
Do not convert curly to straight as an anti-AI measure; that is cosmetic and
can break a house style. Consistency is the only real goal here.

F5. Stray Markdown and broken markup
------------------------------------
Markdown surviving into a format that does not use it (asterisk emphasis,
hash headings, pipe tables in a wiki or a Word document), and malformed markup
generally.

Fix: convert to the target format's own markup. Note that genuinely random
markup errors point away from AI: those come from editors and buggy tools.


G. CHATBOT LEFTOVERS AND RENDER ARTIFACTS
=========================================

Everything in G1 and G2 is deleted, not rewritten. None of it is content.

G1. Conversational leftovers
----------------------------
Text meant as correspondence, advice or prewriting, pasted in with the draft.

Watch: I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, You're absolutely right,
Would you like ..., is there anything else, let me know if, feel free to ask,
more detailed breakdown, Here is a ..., Here's a template for, I hope this
message finds you well, you can copy and paste, as an AI language model, as a
large language model, I'm sorry, I cannot offer medical advice but.

Also delete: instructions addressed to the writer ("ensure the content is
presented in a neutral tone"), meta-commentary on what the section will do
("In this section, we will discuss"), and offers of further help.

G2. Render artifacts by tool
----------------------------
Internal citation and formatting machinery exposed in the output. These are
unambiguous: no human types them.

  - ChatGPT: :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}, oai_citation, oaicite,
    turn0search0 and other turn<N>search<N> tokens, attributableIndex,
    a trailing "+1" standing in for a link.
  - Gemini: [cite: 1], [cite: 3, 12, 13], [span_1](start_span).
  - Grok: grok_card, grok-card data-id, grok_render_citation_card_json.
  - DeepSeek: lenticular brackets with a dagger, as in the pattern
    [full-width bracket] digits dagger L261-269 [closing bracket].
  - Perplexity: [attached_file:1], [web:1], and citations pointing at an
    Amazon S3 URL containing ppl-ai-file-upload.
  - Unclassified: a fenced directive of the form :::writing{variant="document"
    id=NNNNN}, sometimes with matching triple colons at the end.
  - Tracking parameters appended to source URLs: utm_source=chatgpt.com,
    utm_source=openai, utm_source=copilot.com, referrer=grok.com. Gemini and
    Claude add these less often.

Note on the URL parameters: they prove a tool touched the citation, not that
it wrote the prose. Some writers use AI only to find sources.

G3. Unfilled placeholders
-------------------------
Fill-in-the-blank templates the writer forgot to complete: [Describe the
specific section that needs editing], [Specific Topic], [link to the revised
article], [Language, e.g., non-English], [NUMBER], Lorem ipsum.

Fix: fill them, or delete the sentence. Never leave one in a delivered draft.
Placeholders you add deliberately in the form [ADD: ...] are different: they
are a to-do list for the writer, and the skill reports them separately so
they can be resolved before the text ships.

G4. Reference problems to flag, never to guess at
-------------------------------------------------
Fabricated citations are the most damaging AI failure and the one you must not
paper over. Flag each of these for the writer to verify. Do not invent a
replacement, do not "correct" an identifier, and do not delete a citation
merely because it looks odd.

  - Several broken external links in one new document, especially when the
    URLs are not in the Internet Archive either. Watch out for innocent
    explanations: paywalled or library-proxied links that work for others,
    URLs mangled by a tool, and links missing their start or end from a
    copy-paste.
  - Invalid ISBN checksums.
  - Unresolvable DOIs.
  - DOIs that resolve to a real but unrelated article. This is the nastiest
    case, because the citation looks verifiable until someone opens it.
    Check author, year and title together: a plausible-looking reference can
    name an author who was dead at the purported date of writing.
  - Book citations with no page numbers and no URL.
  - Named references defined but never cited in the text.

Fix: mark each as [VERIFY: DOI resolves to a different article] or
[VERIFY: link dead, not in archive] and list them in the change log. If the
writer cannot verify a reference, the claim it supports has to come out.


H. NOT RELIABLE TELLS, AND HOW WRONG EVERYONE IS
================================================

Do not "fix" these. Several of them point the other way, and editing them out
makes writing worse while chasing a signal that was never there.

  - Perfect grammar. Plenty of people write clean prose.
  - Mixed casual and formal register, or prose that is both clinical and
    emotional. This suggests a technical writer, youth, playfulness,
    neurodivergence, or several authors in one document.
  - "Bland" or "robotic" prose. LLM output has specific traits; generic
    flatness is not one of them, and readers unfamiliar with AI writing often
    find real AI text lively.
  - "Fancy", academic or formal prose. LLMs overuse particular words. The
    correlation does not extend to long words or formal registers in general.
  - Transition words in isolation. Only a few are actually overused, and
    essay-style writing has used them forever. Moderate use is fine.
  - Curly quotes on their own (see F4).
  - Unsourced content. Hundreds of thousands of pre-LLM documents lack
    citations, and modern models with web access add citations freely. Their
    citations are often wrong, but they exist.
  - Bizarre or broken markup. Random-looking errors point at editors,
    extensions and conversion bugs, not at LLMs.
  - Correct markup. Getting the formatting right is normal.

How accurate is anybody, including you:
  - Detection tools beat chance but have non-trivial error rates, and they
    degrade under paraphrasing, markup changes, spacing changes, and models
    they were not trained on. A high detector score is not proof.
  - One 2025 study found humans no better than chance at distinguishing LLM
    text. Another, on German theses, found 57 percent recognition of AI text
    and 64 percent of human text.
  - Heavy LLM users reach roughly 90 percent. That still means about one false
    positive in ten confident calls.
  - Human writing is converging with LLM writing. The influence is measurable
    in academic prose, in word choice and semantics, and even in speech from
    2024 onward. The baseline is moving.
  - Text written before 30 November 2022 predates general public access to
    ChatGPT, so AI can be ruled out regardless of how it reads.

Consequence for this skill: describe text as reading as AI-typical. Never
assert who or what wrote it. False accusations cost real people, and you will
be wrong more often than you feel.


I. MODEL-SPECIFIC NOTES
=======================

Every model and version has its own idiolect, and what is typical of GPT-5 is
not typical of GPT-4 or of Gemini. Use these to interpret a draft, not to name
a culprit.

  - ChatGPT: curly quotes; utm_source=chatgpt.com on sources; the oaicite
    family of render artifacts; strong focus on broader context and legacy;
    longer output than Gemini or Claude. GPT-5.1 suppresses em dashes. Likely
    the most widely used model for encyclopedia edits.
  - Claude: as of July 2026, the one contemporary model using em dashes more
    than professional writers; typically straight quotes, not curly; more
    concise than ChatGPT and Grok; less inclined to the broader-context habit.
  - Gemini: [cite: N] artifacts; typically straight quotes; concise; adds
    tracking parameters less often.
  - DeepSeek: curly quotes; lenticular-bracket citation artifacts.
  - Grok: the "Y rather than X" construction; superficially scientific
    vocabulary (causal, empirical, correlate); still overuses underscore in
    2026; grok_card artifacts; referrer=grok.com; and a tendency to broader
    context and to very long articles.
  - Perplexity: [attached_file:N] and [web:N] tags; S3 citation URLs.

One caution that applies to all of these: models are updated constantly, the
vocabulary lists narrow over time, and vendors actively suppress known tells.
Treat every item here as dated evidence rather than a standing rule, and weigh
substance and structure above any of it.
