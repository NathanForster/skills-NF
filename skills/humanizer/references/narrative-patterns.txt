NARRATIVE PATTERNS: AI-TYPICAL CHOICES IN STORY CONSTRUCTION
===========================================================

Source: Russell, Rajendhran, Pham, Iyyer and Wieting, "StoryScope:
Investigating idiosyncrasies in AI fiction", COLM 2026 (arXiv 2604.03136),
paraphrased. Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 5, and appendix Tables 14 to 17.

Read this file before surface-patterns.txt when the text is fiction. The
reason is the central finding below: in fiction, the tells that survive
editing live in the story's construction, not its sentences.


1. WHAT THE STUDY DID
====================

The authors built a pipeline that extracts interpretable, discourse-level
narrative features from a story: things like who drives the resolution,
whether time runs straight, how emotions are conveyed, whether the narrator
spells out the theme. About 300 features across 10 dimensions (plot, agents,
setting, temporal structure, perspective, social networks and others),
deliberately excluding stylistic cues such as word choice and syntax.

Corpus. 10,272 writing prompts, each derived from a published human story
(the human side comes from the Books3 collection of published books). Each
prompt was then written as a story by five models: Claude Sonnet 4.6,
DeepSeek V3.2, Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5.4 and Kimi K2.5. That gives six parallel
versions of every story, about 61,600 in total, each around 5,000 words.

Headline results.
  - Narrative features alone separate human from AI stories at 93.2 percent
    macro-F1, keeping over 97 percent of the performance of models that are
    also allowed to see style.
  - They attribute a story to one of the six sources at 68.4 percent macro-F1,
    against a 16.7 percent chance baseline.
  - A compact core of 30 features carries much of the human-versus-AI signal.
  - The five models cluster together in narrative space, and human stories sit
    in a separate, more spread-out region. The average distance between human
    and AI centroids is 1.6 times the average distance between AI centroids,
    and even the closest human-AI pair is further apart than the most distant
    AI-AI pair.
  - Human stories are rarer. 24.7 percent of human stories fall in the rarest
    10 percent of the corpus, against 7.1 percent of AI stories. For a given
    prompt, the human version is the rarest of the six 57.8 percent of the
    time, where chance would be 16.7 percent.


2. THE RESULT THAT SHAPES THIS COMMAND: SURFACE EDITING BARELY HELPS
===================================================================

The authors asked whether their narrative detector still works after a story
has been edited to remove surface-level AI artifacts. They used LAMP, a
span-level rewriting framework from Chakrabarty et al. (2025) that finds and
rewrites seven categories of AI writing problems, including cliche, redundant
exposition and purple prose, guided by 25 examples from professional writers.
They applied it to 278 Gemini-written stories, with Gemini doing the
rewriting.

After the edit, the narrative classifier still detected the stories at 93.9
percent macro-F1, against 95.5 percent before. A drop of 1.6 points. The
narrative features are close to independent of the prose artifacts that
surface editing targets: cleaning up cliches and purple prose does not change
causal linearity, thematic explicitness or sensory over-description.

What this means for editing. Polishing sentences leaves the story's shape
exactly as AI-typical as it was. If the goal is fiction that reads as
authored, the work is in plot, time, resolution and what the narrator chooses
to explain. Do that first, then fix the prose.

Caveats. Hold these firmly when applying the numbers.
  - Long-form fiction only: stories around 5,000 words. The findings were not
    tested on flash fiction, poetry, screenplays or nonfiction.
  - Specific model versions from 2026. Newer versions will drift, and a
    fingerprint that holds for Claude Sonnet 4.6 may not hold for its
    successor.
  - The human baseline is published fiction from books, so "human" here means
    professionally written and edited work, not typical amateur writing. An
    unpublished human draft can easily look "AI-typical" on these measures.
  - The robustness test used one model family (Gemini), one rewriting method,
    and 278 stories.
  - Features were extracted by an automated pipeline, validated by human
    annotators on a sample.
  - Every number below is a population rate. 52 percent of human narrators
    also state the theme outright. None of these features is wrong in a single
    story. The problem is defaulting to all of them at once, every time.


3. THE 30 CORE FEATURES, IN 7 GROUPS
===================================

From Table 16. Three features (Reference Explicitness, Subplot Integration,
Emotional Expression) appear twice with different options, so the 33 rows
below cover 30 features.

How to read the tables.
  - (1-5) rows are means on a 1-to-5 scale.
  - (ord) rows are means over ordinal codes.
  - (%) rows are the share of stories with that option.
  - The AI column averages all five models.
  - Gap is Human minus AI, computed from the rounded values shown. Negative
    means AI does it more. The paper's own gaps come from unrounded means and
    can differ in the last digit.


3.1 Thematic over-determination  (AI-elevated)
----------------------------------------------
AI spells out what the story means instead of trusting the reader.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Thematic explicitness and moralizing (1-5)             3.28    3.94   -0.66
  Moral / philosophical weighting (1-5)                  3.26    3.68   -0.42
  Thematic unity (1-5)                                   4.41    4.74   -0.33
  Narrator explicitly comments on theme: yes (%)           52      77     -25
  Dialogue used for philosophical debate (%)               34      59     -25
  Intertextual references as implicit echoes (%)           50      72     -22

The paper's example: a grieving character's arc typically ends with the
narrator stating the lesson learned.

Editing moves:
  - Find the sentence where the narrator names the lesson. It is usually in
    the last paragraph or two, sometimes at the end of every scene. Delete it
    and end on the action or image before it.
  - Rewrite dialogue that debates the theme into dialogue where people want
    something concrete from each other: money, an apology, a lift home.
  - Let one thread pull against the theme, or have nothing to do with it.
    Perfect thematic unity is itself a signature.
  - Replace a vague allusion ("like something out of an old myth") with a
    specific named work if the writer has one in mind, or cut it. Do not
    invent the reference; mark [ADD: which work?] if it matters.
  - Let some scenes be about nothing larger than themselves.


3.2 Embodied and sensory overwriting  (AI-elevated)
---------------------------------------------------
AI conveys feeling through bodies and surroundings, almost never by naming it.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Emotion conveyed through the body (%)                    38      81     -43
  Setting as a mirror of inner state (1-5)               3.58    4.07   -0.49
  Environmental and ecological emphasis (1-5)            2.83    3.21   -0.38
  Smell among the dominant senses (%)                      57      82     -25
  Sensory density (1-5)                                  3.66    3.93   -0.27
  Depth of interior access (1-5)                         3.67    3.93   -0.26

The paper's example: where a human writes that a character felt afraid, AI
renders a tightening chest, a cold sweat and the lamplight dimming. Humans
use explicit emotion labels in 29 percent of stories, AI in 8 percent (see
3.7).

Editing moves:
  - Name some emotions plainly. "He was afraid." "She was bored of him." One
    direct label often does more than three bodily metaphors.
  - Keep the physical cue only where it carries something the label cannot.
    Cut the stock set: tight chest, breath she did not know she was holding,
    jaw clenching, heart hammering, stomach dropping.
  - Stop weather and light from tracking mood. Let it rain at the wedding and
    be sunny at the funeral, or leave the weather out.
  - Cut smell unless the smell does work in the plot or characterization.
  - Thin out narrated interiority. Not every beat needs the character's inner
    state explained; some can be left to the reader.


3.3 Structural streamlining  (AI-elevated)
------------------------------------------
AI tells one clean story in one line, driven and resolved by the protagonist.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Causal chain continuity (1-5)                          3.92    4.20   -0.28
  Spatial granularity (ord)                              2.27    2.53   -0.26
  Resolution driven by the protagonist's choice (%)        46      69     -23
  Characters introduced by external description (%)        30      52     -22
  No subplots (%)                                          57      79     -22
  Resolved through internal understanding (%)              27      47     -20
  Opening spatial grounding (ord)                        2.12    2.33   -0.21
  Character investment before the threat (1-5)           2.76    2.99   -0.23

The paper's example: a human mystery might open at the funeral and spiral
backward through decades; AI tells it from the first clue to the grand
reveal.

Editing moves:
  - Break the causal chain somewhere. Let an event happen that the previous
    one did not cause, and let a thread go nowhere.
  - Let the resolution come from outside the protagonist, from luck, another
    person, or circumstance, or leave it partly unresolved. Humans are much
    more comfortable with an ending nobody chose.
  - Replace the acceptance-and-understanding ending ("and she finally
    understood that...") with an event, a decision that costs something, or an
    open question.
  - Add a subplot, or restore one that was cut for tidiness.
  - Introduce people through what they say or do, not through a description
    of their face and clothes.
  - Do not open by establishing place. Start in the middle of something.
  - Bring the threat in earlier. AI spends longer making the reader like a
    character before anything happens to them.


3.4 Named references  (human-elevated)
--------------------------------------
Humans name real things. AI gestures at them.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Intertextual strategy: explicit named reference (%)      47      24     +23
  Reference explicitness: balanced mix (%)                 37      16     +21

AI generally avoids naming real works, authors, brands and places, and sticks
to vague allusion.

Editing moves:
  - Where a character would name the song, book, street, shop or brand, name
    it. Real proper nouns are among the strongest signals of an actual person
    with actual experiences.
  - Mix explicit references with implicit ones rather than using only one kind.
  - Never fabricate a reference, a quote, or a real person's action. If the
    story needs a specific name the writer has not supplied, insert
    [ADD: which band? which street?] and let the writer choose.


3.5 Reader address  (human-elevated)
------------------------------------
Humans acknowledge that someone is reading. AI writes as though nobody is.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Fourth-wall permeability (ord)                         0.67    0.39   +0.28
  Direct reader address (ord)                            0.28    0.07   +0.21

The paper summarizes these as humans breaking the fourth wall far more often
(roughly 67 against 39 percent) and addressing the reader directly roughly
four times as often (28 against 7 percent).

Editing moves:
  - Where the voice supports it, let the narrator acknowledge the telling: an
    aside, a "you", a narrator who admits to not knowing or to shaping the
    story.
  - Use this sparingly and only in voices that would do it. A forced
    "dear reader" dropped into a close third-person story is a new tell, not a
    fix.


3.6 Temporal complexity  (human-elevated)
-----------------------------------------
Humans play with time. AI runs the clock forward.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Recontextualization after a surprise (1-5)             3.28    2.95   +0.33
  Chronological discontinuity (1-5)                      2.40    2.12   +0.28
  Nonlinear framing to delay disclosure (1-5)            1.96    1.68   +0.28
  Anachrony intensity (1-5)                              2.58    2.31   +0.27

Editing moves:
  - Consider opening after the main event and working back to it.
  - Use a flashback or flash-forward to hold back a key fact until it lands
    hardest.
  - When a surprise arrives, let it change how earlier scenes read, and let
    the text register that change.
  - Allow time skips without a transitional paragraph explaining them.


3.7 Narrative diversity  (human-elevated)
-----------------------------------------
Humans range more widely. AI settles into a narrow set of defaults.

  Feature                                               Human     AI     Gap
  Location variety (ord)                                 1.34    1.08   +0.26
  Dialogue-to-narration proportion (1-5)                 2.95    2.70   +0.25
  Subplots that parallel the theme obliquely (%)           42      21     +21
  Protagonist framed as morally ambivalent (%)             59      38     +21
  Emotion conveyed by explicit labels (%)                  29       8     +21

Editing moves:
  - Move the story through more places.
  - Convert some summarized narration into dialogue.
  - Keep subplots, and let them echo the main theme indirectly rather than
    restating it.
  - Let the protagonist do something the story neither endorses nor punishes.
    AI protagonists are mostly good people having an arc.
  - See 3.2 on naming emotions.


4. PER-MODEL FINGERPRINTS
========================

From Section 5 and Table 17. Each source also has features that are rare
elsewhere. Counts of distinctive fingerprint features: Human 32, Claude 26,
GPT 11, Gemini 11, DeepSeek 7, Kimi 3. Per-source attribution accuracy
(macro-F1 with style, then narrative-only): Human 93.0 and 88.5, Claude 89.3
and 77.1, GPT 82.1 and 73.0, and Gemini, DeepSeek and Kimi together between
55 and 67. Gemini, DeepSeek and Kimi are the most confused with each other;
the paper calls them triplets.

Use these to diagnose a draft, not to name its author.

Claude (Sonnet 4.6): "keeps it cool". The most distinctive AI profile.
  Events escalate less than in any other source, event types vary least, and
  the narrative voice is the most uniform. It honors and extends storytelling
  conventions rather than subverting them (62 percent of stories, against 39
  to 56 percent elsewhere). It favors epilogues and flash-forward endings,
  avoids dream sequences, leans to uncanny or haunted moods, and prefers quiet
  endings to "avalanche" ones.
  Fix: let the stakes compound and the events differ in kind. Let the voice
  change register under pressure. Cut the epilogue. Break a convention the
  genre expects instead of honoring it. Consider one structural rupture, a
  dream or a hard time cut.

GPT (5.4): "likes to gossip".
  Gossip and rumor drive the plot (64 percent, against 44 to 55 percent
  elsewhere). It frames stories as reflections on events years or decades
  ago, subverts reader expectations more than other models (41 percent, against
  27 to 36), avoids habitual narration, leaves reconciliations partial, and
  builds ensemble social networks at roughly human levels.
  Fix: replace reported rumor with the scene itself. Move the narrator's
  vantage closer to the events. Use habitual narration ("every Sunday he
  would...") where the life described has routines.

Gemini (3 Flash): tidy and bleak.
  The tidiest endings and the most extended denouements, and the bleakest
  settings (88 percent tagged bleak and oppressive). Also: protagonists whose
  social circle expands, mostly direct speech, siege or ordeal plots, full
  personal names, frequent flashbacks, and characters introduced by external
  description.
  Fix: end sooner and leave something open. Cut the denouement. Let the
  setting be ordinary, mixed, or pleasant. Introduce people through action.

DeepSeek (V3.2): front-loads.
  Gives away crucial context at the start that other sources hold back. Also:
  a visible narrator, emotion shown through behavioral cues, backstory spread
  evenly through the story, and embedded stories told inside scenes.
  Fix: withhold. Let the reader work out the situation. Put backstory where it
  pays off instead of distributing it evenly.

Kimi (K2.5): the generic center.
  The fewest fingerprints and the lowest attribution score. It sits in the
  middle of the AI cluster with no distinctive narrative choices; the few it
  has are introducing characters in action, opening in medias res, and not
  labeling traits explicitly.
  Fix: there is no single quirk to remove. Apply the group moves in section 3,
  because the problem is the absence of any distinctive choice.

Human: the target, and the thing to protect.
  The most distinctive source, and the most varied: human stories are more
  spread out and more often unusual. Top human fingerprints include
  introducing characters in dialogue, a single focal character, back-loaded
  revelation, and crossover-genre ambition.
  Fix: do not sand this off. When editing a human draft, preserve the odd
  structural choices, the unresolved threads and the named specifics. They are
  the part that reads as authored.


5. NONFICTION ANALOGS  (editorial inference; not tested by the paper)
====================================================================

StoryScope studied fiction only. The mapping below is this skill's own
inference: the same habits appear to show up in essays, proposals and
reports, and the same moves appear to help. Treat it as a hypothesis to apply
with judgment, not a finding.

Thematic over-determination.
  Seen as: every example followed by "This shows that...", a closing
  paragraph that states the takeaway, a thesis restated at the end of every
  section.
  Move: end on the strongest specific evidence and let the reader draw the
  conclusion. State the argument once, where it is needed.

Embodied and sensory overwriting.
  Seen as: atmospheric scene-setting openers in reports ("The morning sun
  rose over the factory floor as the team..."), and evaluative color in place
  of measurement.
  Move: lead with the fact or the finding. Keep description only where it is
  evidence.

Structural streamlining.
  Seen as: one clean causal story with no dead ends, no rival explanations
  and a resolution in which the author's recommendation fixes everything.
  Move: include the approach that failed, the alternative explanation you
  ruled out and why, and the question that is still open.

Named references.
  Seen as: "research shows", "studies suggest", "industry leaders".
  Move: name the study, the dataset, the person, the year, or mark
  [ADD: which study?]. This is the single most human signal in expository
  writing, and it is the one that cannot be faked.

Reader address.
  Seen as: a document that never acknowledges its reader's actual decision,
  constraint or likely objection.
  Move: address the decision the reader has to make, and answer the objection
  they will raise.

Temporal complexity.
  Seen as: strict chronology in a report whose reader wants the conclusion
  first.
  Move: lead with the result, then give the history that explains it.

Narrative diversity.
  Seen as: every section the same length and shape, every paragraph the same
  topic-sentence, three-supports, wrap-up pattern.
  Move: vary section length by importance. Let a short paragraph stand alone.
  Let one section be a list and another an argument.
